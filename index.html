How I think

Four things I believe about building products, and the moments that taught me each one.

Users · learned on Zuri

Look for the problem people have learned to live with.

Great products rarely start with a feature request. Before Zuri, I thought the sums I did in my head before opening my banking app were just my habit. Then I asked friends, professionals and parents, and almost everyone did the same. That quiet, everyday workaround was the real problem.

Experience · learned on the hackathon platform

Design for everyone in the room.

Most products have more than one user, and each one deserves a good experience. On the hackathon platform I designed for judges and participants together: one clear rubric with a reason box so judges could score well without friction, private feedback teams could open with the email they already used, and a way for admins to fix a mistyped score without touching a judge's words.

Ownership · learned on StacStart × iDICE

Give the team the problem, then get out of the way.

During the iDICE rebuild I didn't micromanage how engineers solved things. I made the goal and the priorities clear, put the work in Jira where everyone could see it, and let each engineer own their part. Visibility let me trust the team without hovering.

Strategy · learned on iDICE and Mimuads

Decide what not to build yet.

Strategy is mostly sequence. On iDICE, bulk onboarding went first and job-readiness tools waited until learners would need them. At Mimuads, we proved venue hosts would sign up before building the booking engine. "Not yet" is how a small team ships the thing that matters.

A hackathon where nobody leaves with just a number

Most hackathons end with a ranking and silence. 96 teams deserved to know why they placed where they did.

Product Manager. I designed the judging and results system end to end, built around a good experience for judges and participants.

Public ranking, private feedback. Every score needs a written reason, and each team sees only its own.
Evidence
